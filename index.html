📖 LIS211 — Information Literacy

Master Lecture Notes
University of Malawi — Bachelor of Arts in Library and Information Science

1. Meaning of Information Literacy

Information literacy is more than simply finding information in books or on the internet.

An information-literate person is able to:

  • Identify when information is needed
  • Understand what information is needed
  • Locate appropriate information
  • Search effectively
  • Evaluate information
  • Organize information
  • Use information ethically
  • Apply information to problems and situations
  • Create new knowledge from information

Information literacy therefore involves finding, understanding, evaluating, using and creating information.

2. Components of Information Literacy

The major components discussed in LIS211 include:

  1. Identifying information needs
  2. Locating information
  3. Searching for information
  4. Evaluating information
  5. Organizing information
  6. Using information ethically
  7. Creating new knowledge

These components are connected. Information literacy is not always a straight-line process. While searching or evaluating information, a person may discover a new question and return to the information need or search again.

3. Identifying Information Needs

An information need is the gap between what a person already knows and what the person needs to know.

Information may be needed to:

  • Answer a question
  • Solve a problem
  • Make a decision
  • Complete a task
  • Understand an issue
  • Conduct research
  • Develop new knowledge

Examples of Information Needs

Health: A person may need information to understand a health issue or make an informed health decision.

Finances: A person may need financial information to understand how to manage money or make financial decisions.

Employment: A person may need information about available jobs, qualifications, applications and employment opportunities.

Politics: A person may need information about political issues, public policies, leaders, elections and government activities.

Education: A student needs information about courses, assignments, examinations and research.

4. Understanding the Information Need

Before searching, the information need should be clearly understood.

Consider:

  • What exactly do I want to know?
  • What problem am I trying to solve?
  • What question am I answering?
  • What is the scope of the information?
  • What key concepts are involved?
  • How much information do I need?
  • What type of information do I need?
  • Who is the information for?
  • What will the information be used for?

A clearly defined information need makes searching more effective.

5. Locating Information

After identifying an information need, the next step is to locate appropriate information.

Information can be found in:

  • Books
  • Academic journals
  • Newspapers
  • Websites
  • Databases
  • Government publications
  • Reports
  • Archives
  • Institutional repositories
  • Digital libraries
  • Other information resources

Different sources have different strengths and limitations. Therefore, an information user must choose sources according to the information need.

6. Academic Information Databases

Academic databases are important sources for scholarly information.

Examples include:

  • Scopus
  • Web of Science
  • JSTOR
  • EBSCO

Databases can help researchers locate scholarly articles and other academic information. However, finding an article in a database does not automatically mean that the information is suitable. The information must still be evaluated.

7. Searching for Information

Searching is not simply typing words into Google and accepting the first result.

An effective search requires a search strategy.

A search strategy may include:

  • Identifying keywords
  • Identifying important concepts
  • Finding synonyms and alternative terms
  • Using controlled vocabulary
  • Combining search terms
  • Using appropriate databases
  • Applying inclusion criteria
  • Applying exclusion criteria
  • Refining the search
  • Evaluating search results

8. Keywords

Keywords are important words or phrases that represent the main ideas in an information need.

For example, if the topic is:

“The effect of social media on university students' academic performance”

Possible keywords include:

  • Social media
  • University students
  • Academic performance
  • Students
  • Academic achievement

Different keywords can produce different search results. Therefore, choosing appropriate keywords is an important part of information searching.

9. Controlled Vocabulary

A controlled vocabulary is a standardized set of terms used to describe information consistently.

Controlled vocabulary can help users find information even when different authors use different words for the same concept.

It is particularly useful in organized information systems and databases.

10. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

These are conditions that information must meet to be included.

  • Relevant to the research question
  • Published within a selected period
  • Scholarly source
  • Available in a required language

Exclusion Criteria

These are conditions that cause information to be left out.

  • Not relevant
  • Poor-quality source
  • Outside the selected time period
  • Does not address the research question

Inclusion and exclusion criteria help make searching more focused.

11. Refining a Search

Sometimes a search produces too many results.

The search can be narrowed by:

  • Adding more specific keywords
  • Limiting the date
  • Selecting a particular database
  • Selecting a particular type of source
  • Using additional search terms

Sometimes a search produces too few results.

The search can be broadened by:

  • Removing unnecessary terms
  • Using synonyms
  • Using broader concepts
  • Searching another database

Searching is therefore an iterative process.

12. Evaluating Information

Finding information does not mean that the information is automatically reliable. Information must be evaluated before it is used.

Important evaluation considerations include:

  • Credibility
  • Relevance
  • Authority
  • Accuracy
  • Currency
  • Evidence
  • Purpose

13. CRAAP Test

The CRAAP Test is a framework used to evaluate information sources.

  • C — Currency: How recent is the information?
  • R — Relevance: Is the information relevant to the information need?
  • A — Authority: Who created the information?
  • A — Accuracy: Is the information accurate and supported by evidence?
  • P — Purpose: Why was the information created?

Currency

Ask:

  • When was it published?
  • Has it been updated?
  • Is the information current enough for the topic?

Relevance

Ask:

  • Does it answer my question?
  • Is it appropriate for my purpose?
  • Is the level appropriate?

Authority

Consider:

  • Author
  • Qualifications
  • Institution
  • Expertise
  • Publisher

Accuracy

Ask:

  • Is evidence provided?
  • Can the claims be verified?
  • Are there errors?
  • Do reliable sources support the information?

Purpose

The purpose may be to:

  • Inform
  • Educate
  • Persuade
  • Sell
  • Entertain
  • Influence

14. Authority Is Constructed and Contextual

Authority should not simply be accepted because a person, website or institution appears important.

Authority is constructed and contextual.

This means that what makes someone authoritative depends partly on:

  • The subject
  • The situation
  • The purpose
  • The evidence
  • The community
  • The context in which the information is being used

Therefore, information users should consider both the creator and the context of the information.

15. Comparing Information Sources

One source should not always be accepted without comparison. Reliable information can be compared with other independent sources.

Comparing sources can help identify:

  • Agreement
  • Contradictions
  • Errors
  • Missing evidence
  • Different perspectives

This is especially important when information is important or controversial.

16. Information Quality

Information quality refers to how suitable, reliable, accurate and useful information is for a particular purpose.

Quality information should be:

  • Relevant
  • Accurate
  • Reliable
  • Supported by evidence
  • Appropriate for the purpose
  • Sufficiently current where currency matters

The fact that information is repeated many times does not automatically make it true. Information being popular does not automatically make it reliable.

17. Misinformation

Misinformation is false or inaccurate information that is shared without necessarily intending to deceive.

A person may share incorrect information because they believe it is true.

18. Disinformation

Disinformation is false or misleading information that is deliberately created or shared to deceive or mislead people.

The important difference is the element of intentional deception.

19. Deepfakes

Deepfakes are manipulated or artificially generated media that can make people appear to say or do things that they did not actually say or do.

Deepfakes can involve:

  • Video
  • Audio
  • Images

Because digital technologies can create convincing content, information users must verify important information.

20. Artificial Intelligence and Information

Artificial intelligence can generate text, images, audio, video and other digital content.

AI-generated content may appear convincing but can still contain errors or false information.

Therefore, information users should:

  • Question information
  • Check sources
  • Verify important claims
  • Compare independent sources
  • Consider the purpose of the information

AI content should not automatically be accepted simply because it looks professional.

21. Information Overload

Modern society produces enormous amounts of information.

Information comes from:

  • Websites
  • Social media
  • Books
  • Journals
  • News
  • Digital libraries
  • Databases
  • AI systems
  • Government sources
  • Other digital platforms

The large amount of information can create information overload.

Information overload occurs when the amount of available information becomes difficult for a person to process effectively.

Information literacy helps people identify what is relevant and avoid unnecessary information.

22. Organizing Information

Organizing information refers to the systematic arrangement, classification, documentation and management of collected information.

Organizing information helps a person to:

  • Understand information
  • Find information again
  • Compare sources
  • Identify relationships
  • Identify patterns
  • Synthesize evidence
  • Develop arguments
  • Reuse information

23. Information Ethics

Information ethics concerns the responsible and ethical use of information.

Important principles include:

  • Honesty
  • Respect for intellectual ownership
  • Academic integrity
  • Trust
  • Transparency
  • Accountability
  • Responsible use of information

Information should not be deliberately manipulated or misrepresented.

24. Intellectual Ownership

Information and ideas may belong to the people who created them.

Respecting intellectual ownership means recognizing the contribution of creators and acknowledging their work.

Students should therefore properly acknowledge information obtained from other people.

25. Academic Integrity

Academic integrity means being honest and responsible in academic work.

A student should:

  • Use information honestly
  • Give credit to sources
  • Avoid plagiarism
  • Avoid presenting another person's work as their own
  • Provide accurate references
  • Avoid deliberately falsifying information

Academic integrity builds trust in academic work.

26. Plagiarism

Plagiarism occurs when someone presents another person's words, ideas or work as their own without appropriate acknowledgement.

Plagiarism can be avoided by:

  • Citing sources
  • Referencing sources
  • Using quotation marks for direct quotations
  • Paraphrasing properly
  • Acknowledging ideas from other authors

27. Citation and Referencing

Citation gives credit to the source of information or ideas. Referencing provides information that allows readers to identify the source.

Citation and referencing:

  • Support academic integrity
  • Give credit to authors
  • Allow verification
  • Strengthen arguments
  • Show evidence used in academic work

28. Trust, Transparency and Accountability

Trust: Users should be able to rely on information and the people providing it.

Transparency: Information and its sources should be clear enough for people to understand where it came from and how it was produced.

Accountability: People and institutions should take responsibility for information they produce, use or distribute.

29. Critical Thinking

Critical thinking is the careful examination and analysis of information before accepting or using it.

A critical thinker asks:

  • What is the evidence?
  • Who produced this information?
  • Why was it produced?
  • Is the claim supported?
  • Are there other perspectives?
  • Can the information be verified?
  • What might be missing?
  • Does another reliable source support the claim?

Critical thinking prevents people from accepting information simply because it is popular or confidently presented.

30. Information Literacy and Critical Thinking

Information literacy and critical thinking work together.

A person may find information, but critical thinking is needed to determine:

  • Whether it is reliable
  • Whether it is relevant
  • Whether evidence supports it
  • Whether different perspectives exist
  • How it should be used

Therefore, an information-literate person should be an active and critical user of information.

31. Creating New Knowledge

Creating new knowledge is an important part of information literacy.

It does not simply mean copying or summarizing information from different authors.

Creating new knowledge involves:

  1. Finding information
  2. Examining different sources
  3. Comparing information
  4. Synthesizing ideas
  5. Identifying relationships
  6. Identifying gaps in existing knowledge
  7. Applying existing knowledge to a problem
  8. Developing new explanations, arguments, models or conceptual ideas
  9. Communicating the new understanding

32. Synthesis

Synthesis means bringing information and ideas from different sources together to develop a broader understanding.

Example:

Source A + Source B + Source C → Analysis and synthesis → New understanding

Synthesis is different from simply listing what each author said. The student should identify relationships between ideas and develop a meaningful understanding.

33. Identifying Gaps in Knowledge

Existing authors may leave questions unanswered. A knowledge gap is an area where information or understanding is still incomplete.

An information-literate researcher can:

  • Examine existing literature
  • Identify what has already been studied
  • Identify what has not been adequately explained
  • Develop a question around the gap
  • Investigate the issue
  • Contribute new understanding

34. Creating New Explanations and Ideas

New knowledge may take different forms, including:

  • New explanations
  • New arguments
  • New models
  • New interpretations
  • New solutions
  • Conceptual ideas
  • Research findings

Therefore, information literacy contributes to knowledge building.

35. Information Literacy Is Not Linear

Information literacy should not always be viewed as a straight process.

Identify Need → Search → Evaluate → Find New Information → Discover New Question → Search Again → Re-evaluate → Organize → Synthesize → Create Knowledge

The learner may move backwards and forwards between activities. This makes information literacy a non-linear process.

36. Interdisciplinary Integration

Creating new knowledge may require information from different disciplines.

A researcher may combine ideas from different fields to understand a problem.

This requires:

  • Comparing different sources
  • Understanding different perspectives
  • Integrating evidence
  • Identifying relationships
  • Developing a broader interpretation

This is why critical thinking and information literacy are closely connected.

37. SCARA Framework

The SCARA framework presents information work as:

  • Interactive
  • Iterative
  • Constructive

Interactive

The learner interacts with:

  • Information sources
  • Research methods
  • Other people
  • Existing knowledge

Iterative

The process may be repeated several times. A learner may search, evaluate, return to the question, search again and revise their understanding.

Constructive

The learner does not simply collect information. The learner uses information to construct:

  • Understanding
  • Arguments
  • Interpretations
  • Solutions
  • New knowledge

38. Information Literacy as an Interactive Process

Information literacy involves continuous interaction between:

  • The information user
  • Information sources
  • Questions
  • Search strategies
  • Evidence
  • Existing knowledge
  • New knowledge

As the learner discovers new information, the original understanding may change. Therefore, information literacy is an ongoing process.

39. Information Literacy in Everyday Life

Information literacy is not only for university students. People need information in everyday life.

Health

People need reliable information to understand health issues and make informed decisions.

Finances

People need financial information to manage money and make financial decisions.

Employment

People need information about jobs, qualifications and employment opportunities.

Politics

People need information about political issues, policies, leaders and public affairs.

Education

Students need information to study, research and make educational decisions.

40. Information-Rich Classes and Communities

Information-rich classes or communities have access to information and are able to use it effectively.

Information can help communities understand:

  • What is happening around them
  • Public issues
  • Government activities
  • Community problems
  • Their rights and responsibilities

Access to useful information can support informed participation in society.

41. Informed Citizenship

Information literacy contributes to informed citizenship.

An informed citizen can seek and evaluate information before making decisions about public issues.

Information can help citizens understand:

  • Public affairs
  • Government activities
  • Policies
  • Community issues
  • Their rights
  • Their responsibilities

42. Responsible Voting

Information literacy can also support responsible voting.

Voters need reliable information to understand political issues and make their own decisions.

They should not simply depend on:

  • Rumours
  • False information
  • Manipulated content
  • Unverified social media posts

They should evaluate information and verify important claims.

43. Transparency and Accountability

Access to information can contribute to transparency and accountability.

When people have relevant and reliable information, they can better understand what institutions and leaders are doing.

Information can therefore support:

  • Public awareness
  • Accountability
  • Transparency
  • Participation
  • Informed decision-making

44. Identity Theft and Fraud

Information literacy is also important for protecting people from information-related risks such as identity theft and fraud.

Identity theft involves using another person's personal information while pretending to be that person.

Financial fraud involves the use of false or misleading information to obtain money illegally.

People should be careful when sharing personal information.

Examples of information that may need protection include:

  • Passwords
  • Account details
  • Identification information
  • Financial information
  • Personal documents

Users should verify requests for personal information before responding.

45. Responsible Use of Information

Responsible information users should:

  • Verify important information
  • Protect personal information
  • Respect intellectual ownership
  • Cite sources
  • Avoid plagiarism
  • Avoid spreading false information
  • Consider the purpose of information
  • Respect copyright
  • Use reliable sources
  • Think critically before sharing information

46. Information Verification

Verification means checking information before accepting or sharing it.

Verification can involve:

  • Checking the original source
  • Comparing independent reliable sources
  • Checking evidence
  • Checking the date
  • Checking the author
  • Examining the purpose
  • Looking for supporting evidence

Verification is particularly important for information found through social media and other digital platforms.

47. Information Literacy and Problem Solving

Information literacy supports problem solving.

Identify Problem → Identify Information Need → Search → Evaluate → Organize → Analyse → Apply Information → Develop Solution

Good information helps people make better-informed decisions.

48. Information Literacy and Decision Making

Decision making requires appropriate information.

Before making a decision, an information user should consider:

  • What information is needed?
  • Which sources are reliable?
  • What evidence is available?
  • Are there different perspectives?
  • Is the information current?
  • Can the information be verified?

The quality of a decision can depend on the quality and relevance of the information available.

49. Overall Information Literacy Process

1. Identify the information need
↓
2. Define the question or problem
↓
3. Develop a search strategy
↓
4. Locate information
↓
5. Evaluate information
↓
6. Compare and verify sources
↓
7. Organize information
↓
8. Use information ethically
↓
9. Analyse and synthesize information
↓
10. Identify gaps
↓
11. Create new knowledge

Important: This is not always a straight-line process. The learner may return to an earlier stage whenever new information or a new question appears.

50. Final Summary

Information literacy is the ability to recognize an information need, locate appropriate information, search effectively, evaluate information, organize it, use it ethically and create new knowledge.

It requires more than finding information.

Find → Evaluate → Verify → Organize → Use Ethically → Analyse → Synthesize → Create Knowledge

In today's information environment, information literacy is especially important because people face:

  • Information overload
  • Misinformation
  • Disinformation
  • Deepfakes
  • AI-generated content
  • Online fraud
  • Identity theft
  • Unverified social media information

Information literacy therefore supports academic success, research, critical thinking, problem solving, informed decision-making, responsible citizenship and the creation of new knowledge.
