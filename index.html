LIS211 — INFORMATION LITERACY

1. Meaning of Information Literacy

Information literacy is more than simply finding information online or in books.

It involves the ability to identify an information need, locate information, evaluate it, organize it, use it ethically, and create new knowledge.

Simple meaning:

Information literacy means knowing what information you need, where to find it, how to judge whether it is useful and trustworthy, and how to use it responsibly.

2. Components of Information Literacy

The main components are:

  1. Identifying information needs
  2. Locating information
  3. Evaluating information
  4. Organizing information
  5. Using information ethically
  6. Creating new knowledge

3. Identifying Information Needs

Identifying an information need means recognizing the gap between what a person already knows and what the person needs to know.

An information need may come from a question, problem, decision or task.

When identifying an information need, consider:

  • The question or problem
  • The scope of the information needed
  • Key concepts
  • The depth of information required
  • The type of information required

4. Understanding Information Need

Before searching for information, a person should clearly understand what information is required.

A clear information need helps the researcher avoid wasting time and collecting irrelevant information.

5. Locating Information

Locating information means finding information from appropriate sources.

Sources may include books, journals, databases, websites, newspapers, reports and other information resources.

6. Academic Information Databases

Academic databases provide access to scholarly information.

Examples:

  • Scopus
  • Web of Science
  • JSTOR
  • EBSCO

7. Searching for Information

Searching for information involves developing a strategy for finding relevant information.

A good search strategy can save time and improve the quality of search results.

8. Keywords

Keywords are important words or phrases that represent the main ideas in an information need.

Good keywords help a researcher find relevant information.

9. Controlled Vocabulary

Controlled vocabulary uses standardized terms to describe information.

It helps researchers find information even when different authors use different words for the same concept.

10. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria identify the information that should be included in a search.

Exclusion criteria identify information that should not be included.

These criteria help make searching more focused.

11. Refining a Search

Search results may sometimes be too broad or too narrow. The search can therefore be refined.

Refinement can involve adding keywords, removing unnecessary terms, changing search terms or applying filters.

12. Evaluating Information

Evaluation means judging whether information is trustworthy, relevant and useful for the purpose for which it is needed.

Important evaluation issues include:

  • Credibility
  • Relevance
  • Authority
  • Accuracy
  • Currency
  • Evidence
  • Purpose

13. CRAAP Test

The CRAAP Test is a method used to evaluate information.

  • C — Currency: How recent is the information?
  • R — Relevance: Is the information relevant to the topic?
  • A — Authority: Who created the information?
  • A — Accuracy: Is the information correct and supported by evidence?
  • P — Purpose: Why was the information created?

14. Authority Is Constructed and Contextual

Authority is not always fixed. It depends on the context in which information is being used.

ACRL (2016) explains that authority is constructed and contextual.

This means that the authority of a source should be considered according to the purpose and situation.

15. Comparing Information Sources

Information should not always be accepted after looking at only one source.

Reliable independent sources can be compared to determine whether information agrees or differs.

16. Information Quality

Information quality refers to how accurate, reliable, relevant, current and useful information is.

High-quality information should be supported by evidence and should come from appropriate sources.

17. Misinformation

Misinformation is false or inaccurate information that is shared without necessarily intending to cause harm.

A person may share misinformation because they believe the information is true.

18. Disinformation

Disinformation is false information that is deliberately created or shared to deceive or mislead people.

The important difference is the intention to deceive.

19. Deepfakes

Deepfakes are digitally manipulated or generated audio, video or images that can make people appear to say or do things that they did not actually say or do.

Information users therefore need to verify information before accepting or sharing it.

20. AI and Information

Artificial intelligence can be used to create information.

However, information created by AI should still be evaluated because it may contain errors, incomplete information or incorrect references.

Users should verify important information using reliable sources.

21. Information Overload

Information overload occurs when a person receives more information than they can effectively process or use.

Information literacy helps people select relevant and reliable information instead of simply collecting large amounts of information.

22. Organizing Information

Organizing information means systematically arranging, classifying, documenting and managing collected information.

It helps a person synthesize evidence, see relationships, compare perspectives and build coherent arguments.

23. Information Ethics

Information ethics involves using information honestly and responsibly.

It includes honesty, respect for intellectual ownership, academic integrity, trust, transparency and accountability.

24. Intellectual Ownership

Intellectual ownership means recognizing that people can have rights over ideas, writings, research, inventions and other intellectual works.

Users should respect the intellectual work of other people.

25. Academic Integrity

Academic integrity means being honest in academic work.

Students should not present another person's work or ideas as their own.

Academic integrity supports trust in education and research.

26. Plagiarism

Plagiarism is presenting another person's words, ideas or work as one's own without appropriate acknowledgment.

Proper citation and referencing help prevent plagiarism.

27. Citation and Referencing

Citation gives credit to the source of information used in academic work.

Referencing provides information that allows readers to identify and locate the sources used.

28. Trust, Transparency and Accountability

Responsible information use requires trust, transparency and accountability.

  • Trust: Information users should be able to rely on information and its sources.
  • Transparency: Information processes and sources should be clear.
  • Accountability: Information creators and users should take responsibility for how information is produced and used.

29. Critical Thinking

Critical thinking involves carefully examining information, evidence and arguments before accepting a conclusion.

It requires questioning assumptions, comparing evidence and considering different perspectives.

30. Information Literacy and Critical Thinking

Information literacy and critical thinking work together.

A person needs critical thinking to evaluate sources, identify weaknesses in information and decide what information can be trusted.

31. Creating New Knowledge

Creating new knowledge involves using information from different sources to develop new understanding.

A learner does not simply copy what authors have written. The learner studies existing information, identifies gaps, applies knowledge to those gaps and develops new explanations, models or conceptual ideas.

32. Synthesis

Synthesis means bringing information from different sources together to develop a deeper understanding.

It is more than simply summarizing each source separately.

33. Identifying Gaps

A research or information gap is something that is not sufficiently explained, studied or understood in existing information.

Identifying gaps can provide an opportunity for creating new knowledge.

34. Creating New Explanations and Ideas

After identifying a gap, information can be used to develop new explanations, ideas, models or conceptual artifacts.

This is an important part of building knowledge.

35. Information Literacy Is Not Linear

Information literacy is not always a straight process.

A person may move back and forth between identifying an information need, searching, evaluating, organizing and creating knowledge.

36. Interdisciplinary Integration

Information literacy can involve information from different academic disciplines.

Critical thinking helps users connect information from different areas to understand complex problems.

37. SCARA Framework

The SCARA framework describes information literacy as an interactive, iterative and constructive process.

Information literacy involves continuous interaction between the information user, information sources, searching, evaluation, interpretation and knowledge creation.

38. Information Literacy as an Interactive Process

Information literacy involves interaction between people, information, information systems and the environment.

The process can change as new information is discovered.

39. Information Literacy in Everyday Life

Information literacy is useful in everyday life.

People need information when making decisions about education, health, finances, employment, politics and many other areas.

40. Information-Rich Classes and Communities

Information-rich classes and communities can help people become informed about issues happening around them.

Access to reliable information can support learning, participation and responsible decision making.

41. Informed Citizenship

Information literacy can contribute to informed citizenship.

Citizens need reliable information to understand issues, participate in society and make informed decisions.

42. Responsible Voting

Reliable information can help citizens understand issues and candidates before making voting decisions.

Information literacy therefore supports responsible participation in democratic processes.

43. Transparency and Accountability

Access to reliable information can support transparency and accountability.

People can use information to understand what institutions and organizations are doing and to ask appropriate questions.

44. Identity Theft and Fraud

Information literacy is also important for protecting personal information.

People should be careful when sharing passwords, financial information, identification information and other personal details.

Suspicious information should be verified before action is taken.

45. Responsible Use of Information

Information should be used responsibly and ethically.

Users should respect privacy, intellectual ownership, copyright, academic integrity and the rights of others.

46. Verification

Verification means checking information against reliable evidence or independent sources before accepting or sharing it.

Verification is especially important when information is surprising, controversial or potentially harmful.

47. Information Literacy and Problem Solving

Information literacy helps people solve problems by identifying the information required, locating relevant sources, evaluating evidence and applying the information.

48. Information Literacy and Decision Making

Good decisions often depend on good information.

Information literacy helps people collect relevant information, evaluate alternatives and make informed decisions.

49. Overall Information Literacy Process

  1. Identify the information need.
  2. Develop a search strategy.
  3. Locate information.
  4. Evaluate information.
  5. Compare reliable sources.
  6. Organize information.
  7. Use information ethically.
  8. Synthesize information.
  9. Identify gaps.
  10. Create new knowledge.
  11. Review and refine the process.

50. Final Summary

Information Literacy

Information literacy is the ability to identify, locate, evaluate, organize and ethically use information and to create new knowledge.

It involves critical thinking, effective searching, evaluation of information, ethical use, synthesis, identification of gaps and knowledge creation.

LIS214 — DIGITAL LIBRARY MANAGEMENT

1. Introduction to Digital Libraries

A digital library is a library in which information resources are available in digital or electronic form and can be accessed using computers and digital technologies.

Digital libraries are a set of electronic resources and associated technical capabilities for creating, searching, and using information.

A digital library is not simply a collection of files on a computer. It is organized and constructed for a community of users.

The information needs of users help determine:

  • What resources are collected
  • How resources are organized
  • What searching facilities are provided
  • How users access information
  • What technical capabilities are required

2. Why Digital Libraries Developed

The development of digital libraries was connected to changes in information technology and the problems experienced with traditional information resources.

Class Example: UNIMA and Foreign Journals

An example discussed in class was UNIMA's access to foreign journals.

Foreign journals could take a long time to reach Malawi because they were physically sent from the United Kingdom (UK).

This created problems such as:

  • Delays in receiving journals
  • Users waiting for current information
  • Difficulty accessing recent research
  • Dependence on physical transportation

Digital technology helped change this situation because journals could increasingly be made available electronically.

3. Vannevar Bush

One important person discussed in the development of digital information systems was Vannevar Bush.

In 1945, Vannevar Bush described an idea called the Memex.

Memex

The Memex was a proposed device for storing and accessing large amounts of information.

It was designed as a device that could help a person store:

  • Books
  • Records
  • Communications
  • Other information

The importance of the Memex is that it provided an early vision of how information could be stored and accessed electronically.

Important:

The Memex was not a digital library in the modern sense. It was an early idea that influenced later developments in information storage, retrieval and linking.

4. Microfiche

Another technology discussed in class was microfiche.

Microfiche stores reduced photographic images of documents on a sheet of film.

A microfiche reader is used to enlarge the images so that users can read the information.

Importance of Microfiche

Microfiche helped libraries store large amounts of information in a relatively small physical space.

Instead of keeping many large physical documents, information could be reduced and stored on microfiche.

However, users still needed special equipment to read it.

Class idea:

Microfiche shows an earlier stage in the development of information storage before modern digital libraries.

5. Development Towards Digital Libraries

The development of digital libraries did not happen suddenly. It developed through several technological developments.

The lecture discussed developments involving:

  • Vannevar Bush and the Memex
  • Microfiche
  • Computer technology
  • Electronic publishing
  • Hypertext
  • The Internet
  • The World Wide Web

These developments gradually changed how information could be stored, connected, searched and accessed.

6. Hypertext

Hypertext refers to information that contains links connecting one piece of information to another.

Instead of reading information only in a fixed sequence, a user can follow links to related information.

Hypertext became important in the development of digital information systems because it made it possible to connect documents and information resources electronically.

7. Tim Berners-Lee

Another important person discussed in class was Tim Berners-Lee.

He developed the World Wide Web (WWW).

Importance to Digital Libraries

The World Wide Web made it possible for documents and information resources to be connected and accessed through the Internet.

The Web made digital information much easier to:

  • Publish
  • Connect
  • Search
  • Access
  • Share

8. World Wide Web

The World Wide Web is a system of interconnected information resources that can be accessed through the Internet.

For libraries, the Web provided a major opportunity to make information resources available to users beyond the physical library.

A user no longer necessarily had to be physically inside a library to access information.

9. Electronic Publishing

The lecture also discussed the development of electronic publishing.

Publishers began making scholarly information available electronically.

Examples discussed in class:

  • SAGE
  • Elsevier

These publishers provide electronic scholarly journals and other academic information resources.

Electronic publishing contributed to the growth of digital libraries because libraries could provide users with access to electronic scholarly materials.

10. SAGE

SAGE is an academic publisher that provides scholarly information electronically.

For digital libraries, electronic journals from publishers such as SAGE can become part of the resources made available to library users.

Instead of relying only on printed journals, users can access electronic versions.

11. Elsevier

Elsevier is another major academic publisher.

It provides scholarly journals and other research resources electronically.

Digital libraries can provide access to such electronic resources to their user communities, depending on the library's subscriptions and access arrangements.

12. Digital Library as Resources + Capabilities

The lecturer's definition is important for examinations.

Electronic Resources

These are the digital information materials available to users.

Examples include:

  • Electronic journals
  • Electronic books
  • Digital documents
  • Images
  • Audio
  • Video
  • Other digital materials

Technical Capabilities

These are the technologies that allow users to work with the resources.

They include capabilities for:

  • Creating information
  • Searching information
  • Accessing information
  • Using information
  • Managing digital resources

Digital Library = Electronic Resources + Technical Capabilities

13. Digital Library and User Community

A digital library is developed for a particular community of users.

The information needs of the community influence the development of the digital library.

Example: University Digital Library

A university digital library may be designed for:

  • Students
  • Lecturers
  • Researchers
  • Librarians
  • Other university staff

The resources provided should therefore reflect the information needs of these users.

14. Digital Multimedia

Digital libraries can contain different forms of information.

Text

  • Electronic books
  • Electronic journals
  • Reports
  • Research papers

Still Images

  • Photographs
  • Maps
  • Diagrams
  • Scanned documents

Moving Images

  • Videos
  • Recorded lectures
  • Documentaries

Audio

  • Recorded interviews
  • Lectures
  • Music
  • Sound recordings

Therefore, a digital library can contain much more than text.

15. Distributed Access

Digital library resources can be distributed across different locations.

Users can access information through networks such as:

  • The Internet
  • Institutional intranets

This means that the resources do not necessarily have to be physically stored in the same place as the user.

Example:

A UNIMA student may access an electronic journal hosted on a publisher's system in another country.

16. Remote Access

One major advantage of digital libraries is remote access.

A user does not always have to be physically present in the library.

For example, a student can access digital resources:

  • From home
  • From a hostel
  • From a computer laboratory
  • From another location with Internet access

This is different from a traditional printed resource that may require the user to visit the library.

17. 24/7 Access

Digital libraries can provide access to information 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, depending on the availability of the system and Internet connection.

A traditional library may have opening and closing hours.

Digital resources can potentially be accessed outside those physical opening hours.

Class example:

A student studying at 11:00 p.m. can access an electronic journal without waiting for the physical library to open.

18. Searching Digital Information

Searching is an important capability of a digital library.

Users can search for information using:

  • Keywords
  • Titles
  • Authors
  • Subjects
  • Dates
  • Other search fields

This can make it easier to locate specific information from a large collection.

19. Browsing

Digital libraries can also allow users to browse information.

Browsing means looking through available resources or categories to discover information.

Example:

A student may browse:

Library Science → Digital Libraries → Digital Library Management

without already knowing the exact title of a document.

20. Downloading and Saving

Where permitted by the library or publisher's access rights, users may be able to:

  • Download documents
  • Save documents
  • Print documents
  • Copy information for appropriate use

However, users must respect copyright and the conditions of access.

21. Fast Access to Information

Digital libraries can provide information much faster than systems that depend entirely on physical delivery.

Class Example: Foreign Journals

With printed journals:

UK → Physical transportation → Malawi → UNIMA → User

With electronic access:

Publisher's digital system → Internet → User

This can reduce the physical delay associated with receiving information.

22. Current Information

Digital libraries can help users access more current information, particularly when publishers regularly update their electronic collections.

This is important for researchers and students because scholarly work often depends on recent research.

However, digital does not automatically mean current.

A digital document can still be old.

Therefore, users should check the publication date and other evaluation criteria.

26. Short Exam Definition

A digital library is a set of electronic resources and associated technical capabilities for creating, searching and using information, organized for a particular community of users according to their information needs.

27. Final Summary

A digital library is not simply a computer containing documents.

Digital Library = Electronic Resources + Technical Capabilities + Users + Information Needs

The development of digital libraries was influenced by:

Vannevar Bush → Memex → Microfiche → Hypertext → World Wide Web → Electronic Publishing → Digital Libraries

Important benefits include:

  • Remote access
  • 24/7 availability
  • Fast access
  • Searching
  • Browsing
  • Downloading and saving where permitted
  • Access to multimedia
  • Access through Internet and intranet
  • Access to electronic scholarly resources

📚 LIS212 — Records and Archives Management

Records and Archives Management deals with the creation, use, maintenance, preservation and management of records and archives.

🎯 Main Idea

Records are created when people and organisations carry out activities. Some records are kept because they have continuing value as evidence, information or historical memory.

1. Development of Records and Archives

Records and archives have existed for a very long time. Their development can be understood through different historical periods.

  • Early civilisations
  • Medieval period
  • 19th-century emergence of modern archival science
  • 20th-century professional development
  • Development of records management
  • Development of archives in Africa
  • Development of archives in Malawi

2. Records in Early Civilisations

Early societies created records to support administration, government, trade, religion and social activities.

Records could include information about transactions, land, taxation, laws, government activities and important events.

📌 Important Point

The need to keep information is not new. People have always needed ways of recording and preserving information for future use.

3. Medieval Period

During the medieval period, records were produced and kept by institutions such as governments, churches and other authorities.

Written records became important for administration, legal matters, property ownership and religious activities.

4. Emergence of Modern Archival Science

Modern archival science developed particularly during the 19th century.

During this period, there was increasing recognition that records should be managed systematically rather than being kept randomly.

🎓 Class / Examination Point

The 19th century is important because it marked the emergence and development of modern archival principles and professional archival practice.

5. Why the 19th Century Was Important

The growth of governments and organisations resulted in the creation of increasing amounts of records.

This created a need for systematic ways of arranging, preserving and providing access to records.

  • More government administration
  • More records were being created
  • Records needed systematic management
  • Historical records needed preservation
  • Professional archival practices developed

6. Records and Archives in Africa

The development of records and archives in Africa was influenced by traditional systems, colonial administration, missionaries, churches, companies and governments.

Different groups created records as they carried out their activities.

7. Traditional Records in Africa

Before the widespread use of written records, African communities preserved and communicated information through oral and traditional systems.

  • Oral custodians
  • Judicial officers
  • Messengers
  • Drum beats
  • Ceremonies
  • Oral traditions

🇲🇼 Malawi Example

Before 1860, information could be communicated and preserved through oral custodians, judicial officers, messengers, drum beats and ceremonies.

8. Malawi Before 1860

Before 1860, communities in the area now known as Malawi relied heavily on oral methods for communicating and preserving information.

Important information could be passed from one generation to another through people who acted as custodians of knowledge.

📌 Class Example

Judicial officers, messengers, oral custodians, ceremonies and drum communication played roles in communicating information.

9. David Livingstone and 1859

The lecturer referred to David Livingstone's activities in the region in 1859 and his connection with Lake Nyasa.

📅 Remember

1859 — David Livingstone and Lake Nyasa

10. Missionaries and Record Creation

Missionaries were among the groups that created and kept records in Africa.

Their activities generated records relating to religious work, administration, education, communication and interaction with communities.

11. Sierra Leone Example

The lecturer discussed Sierra Leone as an example of the development of records through the activities of different groups.

Missionaries, planters, hunters and companies generated records during their activities.

📜 Class Example

Records associated with the African Leasing Corporation were mentioned in the period around 1881–1890.

12. Missionaries, Planters and Other Groups

Different groups generated different types of records depending on their activities.

  • Missionaries — religious and administrative records
  • Planters — agricultural and business records
  • Hunters — records related to their activities and movements
  • Companies — business and administrative records
  • Government — administrative and official records

13. Malawi Archives History

The history of archives in Malawi developed through different stages, including colonial administration and later national archival development.

The preservation of records became increasingly important as government administration expanded and more records were created.

14. The 1919 Fire

🔥 Important Malawi Example

In 1919, a fire resulted in the loss of records in Malawi.

This demonstrates the importance of proper preservation and protection of records.

Loss of records can result in the loss of administrative, historical and evidential information.

15. Chiefs and Churches as Record Holders

The lecturer also noted that chiefs and churches have held old records.

These records can be important sources of information about communities, institutions and historical activities.

16. Endangered Archives

Some historical records are endangered because of poor storage, environmental conditions, lack of resources and other risks.

Efforts have therefore been made to identify, preserve and digitise endangered historical records.

📚 Class Example

The lecturer discussed the Endangered Archives work associated with the British Library's digital heritage activities.

17. British Library Digital Heritage

The lecturer gave an example involving the British Library and digital heritage projects aimed at preserving endangered historical materials.

Digitisation can help preserve information and make historical materials available for research while reducing dependence on handling fragile originals.

💰 Class Example

The lecturer mentioned an initial pilot involving approximately £10,000 and about three months, followed by a larger project involving approximately £50,000.

18. Central African Archives — 1947

📅 Important Date

1947 — Central African Archives

This forms part of the development of organised archival administration in the region.

19. Federal Archives of Rhodesia and Nyasaland — 1953

📅 Important Date

1953 — Federal Archives of Rhodesia and Nyasaland

The establishment of the Federal Archives was connected with the administrative structures of the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland.

20. Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland

The Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland was an important administrative development in the history of the region.

The Federation brought together territories under a federal administrative structure, which also affected the management and administration of government records.

📌 Records Point

Federal administration generated and required organised records for government activities.

21. End of the Federation

The Federation came to an end in 1963.

When the Federation ended, the territories continued along separate administrative and political paths. This also affected the administration and custody of records.

🎓 Exam Point

Remember the sequence:

1953 → Federal Archives
1963 → Federation ended

22. Malawi Independence

The lecturer discussed the transition from the colonial period to independence and its importance for the development of national administration and records.

🇲🇼 Important Date

1 January 1964 — Malawi independence

23. Why Records Are Important

Records are important because they provide evidence and information about activities and decisions.

  • Evidence of activities
  • Support for administration
  • Legal evidence
  • Historical information
  • Accountability
  • Institutional memory
  • Research

24. Why Archives Are Important

Archives preserve records that have continuing value.

  • They preserve history.
  • They support research.
  • They provide evidence.
  • They support accountability.
  • They preserve institutional memory.
  • They help future generations understand the past.

25. Records and Archives — Difference

Records Archives
Information created or received during activities. Records preserved because of their continuing value.
May still be actively used. Usually preserved for long-term or permanent use.
Support current activities. Support historical, evidential and research needs.
